# The Post-Sale Hinge: Why It's Load-Bearing for the 2027 Ad-Removal Filing

**Date:** 2026-05-28
**Context:** Strategic doctrinal analysis for Filing 2 in the 2027 ownership-track portfolio (see `/2027-ownership-proposals.html`). Filing 2 proposes a §1201 exemption to disable manufacturer-pushed advertising on consumer devices (Samsung Family Hub fridges, Roku autoplay-ad tests, LG webOS sponsored tiles, Echo Show promotional cards, etc.).

The filing is drafted around a specific factual hinge: **the ads at issue must have been pushed onto the device via OTA software update *after* the device was first sold to the consumer.** Devices sold with ads already present (Kindle "Special Offers," some prepaid Android phones with carrier bloatware) are excluded.

This report explains why the post-sale hinge is doctrinally load-bearing — not stylistic — and what would happen to the filing without it.

---

## Question

Why is the "ads pushed via OTA after sale" framing critical to the filing's §1201 viability? Why couldn't the same exemption be drafted to cover devices that came with unwanted on-screen ads from day one?

## Short answer

The post-sale hinge is what makes the filing factually distinguishable from the 2003 Class 9 unskippable-promotional-trailers denial, what gives the proposed class a "qualities of the work" anchor under §1201(a)(1)(C), and what answers the Office's marketplace-alternatives prong. Drop the post-sale hinge and the filing collapses into a doctrinal posture the Office has rejected every cycle it has been tested.

## Four reasons the post-sale hinge is load-bearing

### 1. Adverse-effect inquiry under §1201(a)(1)(B)

The triennial rulemaking is statutorily required to find that absent the exemption, users are "adversely affected in their ability to make noninfringing uses" of the works at issue. The Office's adverse-effect analysis turns on the question *what changed during the exemption period that the user could not avoid?*

- For pre-disclosed ads, there is no cognizable harm during the exemption period. The buyer knew what they were buying at point of sale and chose to buy it anyway. The harm — exposure to ads — was priced into the purchase decision and consented to.
- For post-sale OTA ads, the harm is the unilateral material change to a product already paid for. The "what changed?" question has a clean answer: the manufacturer altered the bargain after the consumer had no exit.

The Office's adverse-effect framing only finds harm when the user could not have avoided it through purchase choice. The post-sale hinge is the factual anchor for cognizable harm.

### 2. Marketplace-alternatives prong

§1201(a)(1)(C)(iv) directs the Register to weigh the effect on the market for or value of copyrighted works, and the Office's standing practice considers whether marketplace alternatives exist. Exemptions have been repeatedly denied where the Office finds that a non-circumventing alternative is available in the marketplace.

- **Pre-sale-disclosed ads:** the alternative is "buy a different device." For ad-tier products like Kindle Special Offers, the same vendor often sells an ads-free SKU at a higher price. The parking-lot section of `2027-ownership-proposals.html` already excludes Kindle Special Offers on this exact ground — the ad tier is pre-disclosed and priced lower, so the consumer who wanted no ads had a clear marketplace alternative.
- **Post-sale OTA ads:** by the time the consumer knows about the ads, the purchase decision is already made and the alternative is gone. The marketplace-alternatives prong is satisfied affirmatively: the consumer *did* choose an ad-free product at point of sale; the manufacturer subsequently removed that ad-free status without consent.

This is the strongest single argument for the post-sale hinge. It tracks the same logic the Office used in 2024 to credit the vehicle-data class — the consumer paid for a device with a particular data posture, and the OEM closed access to that data later.

### 3. The 2003 Class 9 denial maps directly onto pre-sale ads

Class 9 in the 2003 cycle proposed an exemption for circumventing access controls to skip unskippable promotional trailers on DVDs. It was denied. The 2003 Register's Recommendation at PDF pp. 109–115 rested on three grounds:

1. UOP (User Operation Prohibition) was held not to be an "access control" within the meaning of §1201(a);
2. Fast-forward could be re-enabled without circumventing the access control itself;
3. The inconvenience was de minimis.

The factual posture of 2003 Class 9 was: studios baked promotional content into discs *before sale*. Consumers chose to buy or not buy those discs. The Register treated the harm as a known feature of the product at point of sale, hence the de minimis finding.

A 2027 filing covering pre-sale ads would be doctrinally indistinguishable from Class 9. Opposition (CTA, IAB, Joint Creators) would cite the 2003 denial as on-all-fours precedent — and they would be substantively correct. The 2010 Recommendation at PDF p. 43 recycles the same rationale, listing "avoiding access to unwanted material on DVDs, such as the unskippable promotional trailers" as a recurring DVD-access category. A pre-sale-inclusive 2027 filing inherits both denials directly.

The post-sale OTA framing is what stops the 2003 precedent from applying. The factual differentiator is that in 2003, the ads were on the product when the consumer chose to buy it; in the 2027 post-sale scenario, the ads were *added by the manufacturer after the consumer had completed the purchase and could not return the product without loss*. The Office can credit this distinction without disturbing the 2003 holding.

### 4. Class-definition / threshold doctrine under §1201(a)(1)(C)

§1201(a)(1)(C) requires a "particular class of copyrighted works" defined by qualities of the work itself, not by use. Petitions framed by user purpose ("all works for noninfringing uses," "all works for educators") have been denied in every cycle they have been tested (2000, 2003, 2006, 2012) — see `CLAUDE.md` lesson 9, the most-rejected pattern in §1201 history.

- **With the post-sale hinge:** "Computer programs that operate consumer devices and that the manufacturer has caused to display advertising content *not present at the time of sale*" is at least defensibly defined by a quality of the work — namely, the firmware has been unilaterally modified by the manufacturer in a specified way after a defined event (sale). The anchor is the firmware's modification state, not the user's preference.
- **Without the post-sale hinge:** the class collapses to "computer programs operating consumer devices that display advertising the user does not want." That is prima facie a use-defined class — the qualifier "the user does not want" is what's doing all the work, and it sits squarely in the doctrinal pattern the Office has rejected six times across nine cycles.

The post-sale hinge is the work-quality anchor that makes the class survivable on threshold review. Drop it and the petition is denied at threshold, before the merits are reached.

## Could the filing be expanded to cover pre-sale ads anyway?

Substantively, yes — but it would dominate the filing's risk profile and probably sink the whole petition. Three options exist:

**Option A — Single filing covering both pre-sale and post-sale ads.** Highest-risk option. The 2003 Class 9 precedent applies directly to the pre-sale portion; the threshold-doctrine problem under §1201(a)(1)(C) becomes acute; the marketplace-alternatives prong fails for any product category where ad-free alternatives exist. The Register would likely deny the entire class and the denial would be cited against future post-sale-only filings.

**Option B — Split filings, file both.** Less catastrophic than Option A, but the pre-sale half draws a denial that creates an unhelpful precedent on the record. Not recommended for 2027.

**Option C — Post-sale-only for 2027; defer pre-sale to 2030 with a different doctrinal vehicle.** Recommended. The pre-sale ad question is a genuinely different doctrinal problem and needs a different legal vehicle. The two most-plausible 2030 vehicles are:

1. **§1201(i) statutory privacy exception** — pre-sale-disclosed ads frequently imply ad-targeting telemetry whose data flows were *not* disclosed at sale. If the harm is reframed as undisclosed PII collection rather than the ad display itself, §1201(i) is at least a partial fit. The petitioner would need a thorough technical record showing what's collected vs. what's disclosed.
2. **Adhesion-of-options market-power theory** — a record showing that across an entire product category every commercially available alternative carries pre-sale ads, with no ad-free alternative at any price point, would arguably defeat the marketplace-alternatives prong. This is an evidentiary bar significantly higher than anything the Office has previously credited and would require coordinated record-building across a product category (smart TVs, perhaps).

Neither is petition-ready today. Both would benefit from being raised in 2030 *after* the 2027 post-sale filing has established a precedent for the firmware-modification copyright posture.

## Strategic implication

The post-sale hinge is the filing's single most important doctrinal anchor. The Filing 2 record-building plan should make the post-sale-vs-pre-sale distinction visible in the operative class text, not just the brief — class-text language matters more than argument because the Office cabins exemptions to the codified text, and opposition will collapse a distinction that lives only in the brief.

Specifically, the operative class language should retain the phrase "*advertising content that the manufacturer or its successor causes the device to display on the device's own user interface through a software update or remote-service push delivered after the device was first sold to the consumer*." That phrase is the entire substantive innovation of the filing relative to 2003 Class 9. It must be in the regulation, not just the petition.

## What is not analyzed here

- The §1201(i) statutory privacy exception's actual technical scope. The "sole effect" and "sole purpose" prongs are narrow and have not been judicially tested; treatment in this report is brief and indicative.
- FTC consumer-protection doctrine on post-sale unilateral material modification. The analogy to FTC Act §5 unfair-practices doctrine is mentioned obliquely; it is not §1201 doctrine and would be relevant only to a federal-agency support letter.
- The Kindle Special Offers SKU economics. Treated here as a paradigm case of a marketplace alternative, but the underlying pricing and consumer-choice data are not in the §1201 archive and have not been independently sourced.
