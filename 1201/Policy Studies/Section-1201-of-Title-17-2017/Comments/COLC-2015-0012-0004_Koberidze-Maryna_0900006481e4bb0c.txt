SUGGESTIONS  
Past seventeen years under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (“DMCA”)5 have shown that 
the section 1201 rulemaking mechanism plays an important ro le in the digital landscape.6 While 
the mechanism has enjoyed some success,7 several fundamental problems have surfaced in the past 
rulemakings.8 These problems include, but are not limited to, the standard of review for renewals, 
third party assistance  to exempted users , and enforcement of statutory and rulemaking  
exemptions.9 (For a more extensive discussion of these issues, please see Exhibit A, pp. 263-72).10 
1. Section 1201 should be adjusted to provide for presumptive renewal of pr eviously 
granted exemptions (subjects of inquiry 3-5)11 
One of the key problems with the section 1201  rulemaking is the standard of review for 
renewals of previously granted exemptions.12 Due to de novo requirement, proponents who seek 
renewals must present their case before the Register of Copyrights (the “Register”) and the 
Librarian of Congress (the “Librarian”) anew in every rulemaking.13 This is unduly burdensome, 
especially where proponents merely petition for readoption of existing exemptions and there is no 
sound opposition to such an extension.14 To remedy the situation, section 1201 should be adjusted 
to differentiate the  standard of review for petitions that request new exemptions, renewal or 
expansion of (or modification to) existing ones.15 Specifically, section 1201 could be amended to 
create a presumption for recurring exemptions.16 For instance, where the same class of works  or

3. Congress should consider establishing permanent statutory exemption for blind and 
visually impaired e-book readers (subjects of inquiry 9-10)26 
Under current law, neither the Chafee Amendment, nor the fair use provisions protect visually 
impaired users against the DMCA liability if they circumvent technological measures that prevent 
access to the copyrighted material they are allowed to copy.27 And this is despite the United States’ 
international obligations that require facilitation of accessibility to published works for consumers 
who are blind, visually impaired, or otherwise print disabled. 28 Thus, a lthough the e -book 
exemption has been repeatedly adopted by the Librarian since 2003, visually impaired readers 
must continue to seek its renewal in every single rulemaking. 29 To resolve this unfairness, 
Congress could establish permanent statutory exemption for blind, visually impaired, or otherwise 
print disabled readers. Such an exemption would, among other things, help to comply with the 
Marrakesh Treaty.30 (For a more extensive discussion of this issue, please see Exhibit A, pp. 264-
68, 277).

CONCLUSION 
Past rulemakings have demonstrated that although the section 1201 mechanism has certain 
drawbacks, it is not without advances towards balancing between copyright and digital 
technologies.36 But the time has come to adjust this mechanism to make it more up-to-date with 
currently emerging technological developments, as well as  to ensure a greater flexibility and 
adaptability of the copyright system in the future.37 
I respectfully applaud the Copyright Office for undertaking the  study to assess the operation 
and effectiveness of section 1201. Not only will this study provide an invaluable public input, but 
will also assist the Congress in deciding how the section 1201 and the rulemaking process can be 
redefined to better balance competing interests  of the copyright holders and the public in the 
changed digital environment. I am grateful for the opportunity to comment on section 1201. 
Thank you for your time and kind consideration.

ABSTRACT 
 
This Article analyzes seventeen years under the Digital 
Millennium Copyright Act (“DMCA ”) rulemaking 
mechanism and suggests changes to reinforce its successes 
while remedying its failures. Part I briefly discusses the 
legislative history of the rulemaking mechanism and policy 
justifications for its adoption within the DMCA scheme. 
Part II reviews legal and evidentiary standards of the 
rulemaking and
 recent changes to its administrative 
procedure. Part III provides an overview of the prior 
rulemakings and their impact on non-infringing uses, with 
a particular focus on the “e-book” and “cellphone 
unlocking” exemptions. Part IV applauds the Breaking 
Down Barriers to Innovation Act of 2015—which suggests 
numerous improvements to the rulemaking mechanism—
and then recommends other possible changes. Part V 
emphasizes the continued need for the rulemaking 
mechanism, but concludes with some modifications and 
restructuring.

“The advancement and diffusion of knowledge is the only guardian 
of true liberty.”

In 1998, Congress passed the Digital Millennium Copyright 
Act (“DMCA”) to provide greater protection to copyright owners 
against unauthorized access and copying of their works. 2 The Act 
prohibits circumventing technological protection measures 
(“TPMs”) and trafficking in tools that circumvent TPMs, b ut also 
provides seven permanent exemptions to these prohibitions. 3 
Additionally, the DMCA institutes a periodic rulemaking 
proceeding to determine whether to grant additional exemptions 
from the DMCA prohibitions.

compliance with the World Intellectual Property Organization’s

IV. SOLUTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT 
 
The past seventeen years under the DMCA have demonstrated 
that the rulemaking mechanism has its ups and downs, but we must 
keep it for better or for worse. Even those who have voiced against 
this mechanism have at some point acknowledged its legacy and 
importance.
319 No regulatory mechanism is perfect and there is 
always room for improvement. And the DMCA mechanism is no t 
an exception. But it does not mean that the mechanism should be 
abandoned altogether as some consumer advocacy groups have 
repeatedly suggested.
320 Rather, it needs some improvement, a 
second life.  
Recent developments indicate that Congress, the Copyright 
Office, and the public have finally ripen ed to make such 
improvements. Although the number of opportunities is endless, it 
is important to only focus on workable solutions that will be 
welcomed, or at least tolerated, by both sides—
the copyright 
holders and the public at large (represented by consumer advocacy 
groups, educational, scientific research and similar institutions).

CONCLUSION 
 
In sum, as seen from the previous rulemakings, there is a 
positive trend for the industry—rulemaking exemptions are 
becoming broader both in number and scope. 363 If such an 
expansion continues, modified rules of administrative procedure 
prove to be more comprehensive and less burdensome, more 
authority is vested in the Copyright Office, and the precedential 
effect is given to recurring exemptions, the DMCA rulemaking 
mechanism has a good chance for survival and evolvement.
364 Not 
only will such changes make the DMCA scheme more up- to-date 
with the currently emerging technological advances, but will also 
ensure a greater flexibility and adaptability of copyright law in the 
future.
365 
Given the problems highlighted in this Article, it is time for 
Congress to finally do something about the DMCA rulemaking. 
Despite Congress’ intentions, the DMCA provisions continue to tip 
the balance in favor of the interests of copyright holders,
 while 
adversely affecting the interests of the public at large . Not only 
does it happen because of the broad DMCA prohibitions and 
drawbacks of the rulemaking proceeding, but also because over the 
last several decades the copyright holders have grown to be a 
powerful and influential community with strong and successful 
lobbying.
