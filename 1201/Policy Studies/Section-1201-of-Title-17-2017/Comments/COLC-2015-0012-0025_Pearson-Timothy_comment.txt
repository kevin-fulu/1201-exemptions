Comment ID: COLC-2015-0012-0025
Title: Initial Comments - Timothy Pearson
Posted: 2016-03-04T05:00:00Z
Submitter: Pearson-Timothy

I represent a small engineering firm which specializes in embedded systems and firmware development.  The DMCA has severely hindered our competitiveness compared to similar companies in the EU and Asia by making the reverse engineering and analysis of most modern technology devices illegal in the United States, whereas these same activities are not only legal but encouraged overseas.  As a result we have had to abandon entire markets, which when multiplied across all the affected engineering firms in the United States has a significant negative impact on the US economy.<br/><br/>As US citizens are prevented from analyzing and modifying protected devices, fewer and fewer people know how these devices even work, let alone how to innovate in these fields.  Some of these effects can already be seen; the anti-reverse-engineering clause and provisions against software ownership have ironically made it so that US computers and systems are some of the least secure in the world.  This is due to the simple fact that most people have no idea how their proprietary software works (it is illegal to analyze operation of said software due to the DMCA), and therefore cannot anticipate vulnerabilities that citizens of other nations can and do.<br/><br/>Furthermore, due to the restrictions present in the DMCA, security-conscious firms, such as those with long-running research and development programs, cannot purchase or lease proprietary software, as it would be illegal to reverse engineer or analyze the operation of said software while performing any necessary security audits.  Instead, these firms end up turning to fully open-source software or worse, abandoning entire markets where the requisite software does not already exist, in both cases further damaging the US economy as these artificial restrictions are not present in other nations the US trades with.<br/><br/>Finally, it should be noted that the DMCA has encouraged content producers to have their cake and eat it too; now that both effectively perpetual copyright and technical copy restrictions are available, we have largely moved to a pay per view model for critical scientific, technical, and cultural information.  This has resulted in a rise in freely licensed content in an attempt to replace copyrightable material, however where said material is not available outside of a pay-per-view model under the DMCA (for example in the scientific arena) non-US citizens have a distinct advantage in their respective fields.  In either, case once again the US economy is needlessly damaged in vital areas.<br/><br/>Speaking personally, there are serious issues with US cultural heritage being lost over the 120+ year copyright term, and it is likely that historians will have a very biased view of the 21st century which is based on the few surviving, non-DRM protected works.  As most of the preeminent artists of our time have allowed their works to be locked with DRM, the best accomplishments of the last century may well be lost forever, and we may well be remembered as the century that failed to produce anything of note in books, film, or music.  However, if copyright were made explicitly conditional on the intrinsic social contract that a work become publicly owned after the copyright period, and if fair use of DRM protected works (e.g. for archival) was explicitly allowed (including breaking copy protection), these concerns would largely disappear.
