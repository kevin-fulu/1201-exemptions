Title: Initial Reply Comments - Ethan Blocher-Smith

To say that the DMCA is weighted heavily in corporate interests favor is like saying the iceberg beat the Titanic: it is absolutely true, and it doesn&#39;t even come close to describing the true horrifying nature of the behavior. <br/><br/>The DMCA was well intentioned when it began. DVD burning rings, the threats of Napster and other P2P sites, and the beginnings of other problems made the RIAA, MPAA, and other big organizations scared for their profits and intellectual property. To protect these legitimate rights, the DMCA was drafted, and it functioned for a while. <br/><br/>With the rise of online sharing sites and other web storage (i.e. youtube, friendster, myspace, etc.) allowing any user to post and upload any thing, the safe harbor clause was designed, so that well-intentioned sites would not be unduly punished for the illegal behavior, so long as they complied with any. takedown notices. This also was good.<br/><br/>Then the floodgates opened.<br/><br/>The internet became nearly ubiquitous. Cloud hosting, social media, youtube, and a rotating door of sites that held content exploded onto the scene and have been growing ever since. The amount of videos that were uploaded rapidly exceeded the allocated resources the companies had for human review. And so, to remain in compliance with DMCA and the multitude of lawsuit threats from the corporate interests, the automated review was born. Here the problems began.<br/><br/>These automated programs searched a database of every copyrighted piece of content that was added to their list and compared them against each video. Since it was done by computer, it could easily meet demand simply by allocating sufficient server time. Since it did direct comparison, it should only match when copyrighted material came up. And, since every instance it found must be copyright infringement, flagging every single hit must be okay. This was perfectly sensible if every one of those points was true. They are not.<br/><br/>The failure comes in that final point - assuming every single hit is copyright infringement. A simple principle, known as fair use, contradicts this idea. This content that may generate a hit from the automated searches, such as video game playthroughs (Lets Plays from PewDiePie, AngryJoe, etc.), anime abridged series (DBZA, Yu-Gi-Oh Abridged, and many more), movie and TV show reviews/retrospectives (Channel Awesome, Crypt TV, and many more), and many other categories, uses copyrighted material as a base, but perform sufficient additional content with it to make it an entirely new transformative work. This new work, using the copyrighted material in a limited fashion, and for the purpose of either commentary/criticism or parody, is protected speech under the fair use doctrine. <br/><br/>Despite repeated argumentation from the MPAA, RIAA, and other organizations, the fair use doctrine has been upheld in court (see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music Inc; Sofa Entertainment, Inc. v. Dodger Productions, Inc.; Faulkner Literary Rights, LLC v. Sony Pictures Classics, Inc.;  Patrick Cariou v. Richard Prince; Brownmark Films, LLC v. Comedy Partners; and  Seltzer v. Green Day, Inc.). Even if you as the copyright holder of the original being parodied or commented on dislike the final product (scratch that, especially if you dislike it), this does not give you the right to take down the content. Yet that is precisely what the current system allows.<br/><br/>Companies indiscriminately send takedowns for acceptable content protected under fair use and for legitimate offenders, without any attempt to determine which camp the content falls into. The websites hosting this content immediately take it down, since they can&#39;t risk losing safe harbor protection even if the content was protected by law. Citizens who follow law and are well within their private rights have their content taken down repeatedly - see the recent #WTFU campaign, where hundreds of youtubers who make some or all of their profit from their use of fair use content see themselves unfairly and inaccurately censored, penalized, and shut down, all in violation of the law and without recompense.<br/><br/>At the same time, a cottage industry has sprung up, with companies buying copyright to content and specifically claiming any video that contains it as a way to make money, regardless of the legality of the videos. This same method has been used by dubious claimants with no rights to anything to try and censor critics they dislike (see TheJimquisition and all of the terribly illegal things Digital Homicide has done to his videos - seriously, watch his video on it and you will see). The current system allows censoring of any speech, removal of videos to try and strangle competition, and many more problems.<br/><br/>Protecting copyrighted works is a good thing, but companies are currently allowed to abuse the system. Impose statutory damages for companies that abuse the DMCA takedown process or fail to adhere to any fair use review. Limit the power of copyright trolls. Protect free speech. Please.
