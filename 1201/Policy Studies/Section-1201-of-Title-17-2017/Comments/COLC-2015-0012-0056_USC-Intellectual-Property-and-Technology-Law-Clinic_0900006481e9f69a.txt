Introduction  Despite the fact that the DMCA restrictions strengthened and extended the scope of copyrights through “regulating conduct which traditionally has fallen outside the regulatory sphere of intellectual property law,”2 the overarching constitutionally mandated purpose governing copyrights remains to promote the progress of arts and sciences,3 to the benefit of innovation users.  As a sector of copyright law, the DMCA rulemaking process should adhere to this guiding purpose.  The congressional intent underlying the creation of the Office’s exemption rulemaking proceedings was to protect non-infringing uses of copyrighted works from the adverse effects of the DMCA prohibition.4  When addressing the risks that the DMCA posed to copyright users, the House Committee on Commerce expressly affirmed its commitment to maintaining and prioritizing fair use of copyrights.5   It is within this context, with the priority of protecting non-infringing uses, that the Office was given its rulemaking responsibility.  The rulemaking proceeding was to serve as a “fail-safe mechanism,” ensuring that the DMCA restrictions do not impede upon that ability to engage in non-infringing uses.6  Yet, as these comments will show, the current de novo review for exemption renewals and the prohibition of third party-assistance to exemption recipients places too heavy a burden on non-infringing users, effectively allowing the DMCA to restrict the fair use of these copyrights.7   II. B. The Copyright Office is Authorized to Adjust Existing Process to Presumptively Renew Exemptions  The Office should not wait for Congressional action to adjust the renewal process. While the Office relies on a Commerce Committee Report to support its conclusion that the rulemaking should be conducted de novo,34 the reliance on this report is misplaced.  Notably, the draft of the bill discussed in the Commerce Report differs from the final version of the bill that became the DMCA.35  As enacted, the DMCA does not mandate a standard of review in connection with the renewal of previously granted exemptions.36  Given the statutory silence on renewal procedure, as well as principles of administrative law, statutory interpretation, and policy considerations, the Office has substantial support to justify exercising its discretion to presumptively renew exemptions.    1. The Ability to Adjust the Rulemaking Process is Within the Office’s Administrative Powers  Unilateral action by the Office is appropriate because it would further the policy objectives of administrative law.  When Congress confers rulemaking authority, as it conferred to the Office,  it does so to “facilitate[] the continuous operation of the national government by allowing Congress, when enacting a statute, to remove itself from the task of predetermining possible problems that may later arise.”37  Specifically, Congress will grant “rulemaking authority” to an administrative body as a form of “legislative power.”38    Supreme Court precedent supports the proposition that administrative agencies has the benefit of broad discretion in deciding how to conduct its their own rulemaking proceedings. In Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., the Court held that the Atomic Energy Commission did not exceed its statutory authority due to inadequate rulemaking when it granted a license to operate a nuclear power plant.39  The Court emphasized that it would not be proper to “explore the procedural format or to impose upon the agency its own notion of which procedures are ‘best’ or most likely to further some vague, undefined public good.”40       Here, the Office, on behalf of the Library of Congress, is in a better position than Congress to structure the rulemaking process for exemptions to the DMCA. The Congressional intent was to grant deference to the rulemaking authority embodied in the Office and the Librarian. Reliance upon this Commerce Committee report is misguided; the plain language of the statute expresses that the Librarian “shall make the determination [of DMCA exemptions] in a rulemaking proceeding.”49  The statute does not restrict the Office’s authority to choose an evidentiary standard for exemptions, much less the standard as applied to renewals, so it is unnecessary to conjure any such standard using extrinsic evidence.  Even the substantive aspects of the rulemaking as laid out in the DMCA defer to the Librarian’s authority, adding a catch-all of “other such factors as the Librarian considers appropriate.”50  As stated in Vermont Yankee, an administrative body acts properly as long as it “employ[s] at least the statutory minima,” even if the body goes beyond the statute by filling in gaps left by silence on “procedural devices.”51    The Commerce Committee report is also undeserving of influence due to the fact that the report’s discussion of the exemption process is not consistent with the DMCA as enacted.  For example, the report stated that rulemaking proceedings would occur on a “biennial basis,”52 which was later changed to a triennial basis in the bill’s final version.  Since the Commerce Report discussed a different version of the bill, prior to reconciliation by the House and Senate,53 the report is not a trustworthy source for guidance on the rulemaking procedure.  Even if the Commerce Report had merit for statutory interpretation, it would show that presumptive renewal of DMCA exemptions is “consistent with the intent of Congress.”54  The report states the purpose of the DMCA to prevent “the adoption of business models that depend upon restricting distribution and availability, rather than upon maximizing it.”55  Shifting the burden of proof to opponents seeking to nullify existing exemptions would serve that goal.  In particular, farm equipment manufacturers that opposed the 2015-granted exemption for self-repair of motor vehicles seek to “restrict[] distribution and availability” of repair services by excluding all other parties from being authorized repairers.56  III. B. Absent action by Copyright Office, Congress Should Amend Rulemaking Process to Expressly Allow Exemptions Permitting Third-Party Assistance  The Office is authorized to recommend exemptions that permit third party assistance when justified by the record to ensure that the rulemaking process continues to serve as “a barometer for policy concerns and policy action beyond the confines of the statute.”95		Should the Office continue to exclude third party assistance from its recommendations,	Congress should amend the rulemaking process to expressly allow the Office to recommend exemptions that permit third party assistance. As discussed, the need to allow certain exemptions to include third party assistance is particularly salient in regard to the 2015 exemption yet preventing third parties from providing assistance “on behalf of” the owners.  As most farmers rely upon third parties to diagnose and repair their technologically complex agricultural machinery, there is often no feasible alternative to third party circumvention.96  Due to the inefficiencies of the rulemaking process as it stands, even when an exemption is granted, it has “been whittled down by the Copyright Office until it’s so narrow as to become functionally useless.”97   Ultimately, the DMCA exemption process should not stand as a threat to those who seek to use protected works for non-infringing purposes.
