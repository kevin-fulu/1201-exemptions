This approach, expressed in the Unlocking Technology Act of 2015,4 would address nearly all of the problems the Office has identified in this policy study. Therefore, the most straightforward recommendation the Office could give would be to urge Congress to pass the Unlocking Technology Act.  If the Copyright Office chooses instead to recommend a piecemeal approach, it should nonetheless adhere to one overarching principles: simplicity.  One of the reasons the existing permanent exemptions are inadequate to protect lawful and important activities is that they are difficult to rely on in everyday practice, particularly for those who cannot afford regular review by a specialist attorney. The permanent exemptions are replete with conditions that are difficult to satisfy ex ante or whose satisfaction cannot be guaranteed. These conditions act as traps for the unwary, discouraging lawful and important activities.  To mitigate that problem, any proposed exemptions should be easy to understand and apply, so that amateur and independent creators, small businesses, researchers, hobbyists, repair professionals, and other individuals can make lawful uses of copyrighted works with confidence. In addition, Congress should avoid statutory language that attempts to address policy or regulatory issues that are far afield from the purposes of copyright law, such as product safety, automotive design, disclosure of security vulnerabilities, or effectively expands copyright’s reach beyond its traditional contours to protect business models from competition. These policy concerns are the subject of other laws and regulations enforced by expert agencies, or in the courts through tort and commercial law. The DMCA need not address these concerns, and should not duplicate or be contingent upon the application of other laws.   Our comments on the Office’s specific proposals follow.  1.  Assistive Technologies  The Office has proposed using the language of the 2015 triennial exemption regarding screen readers and other assistive technologies for blind and print-disabled persons as the basis for a permanent statutory exemption. Such an exemption would be an improvement over the status quo. Accessibility advocates must expend significant resources every three years to maintain a temporary exemption through the rulemaking process, despite the exception being unopposed across several rulemaking cycles. If they fail to do so, the Copyright Office may recommend that the Librarian deny the exemption (as occurred in 2010). The substantial adverse impact of Section 1201 on accessibility has not varied significantly in the 18 years since passage of the DMCA, and nothing suggests it will change in the foreseeable future. The Office should also consider recommending a permanent exemption covering all lawfully obtained literary works, without the “remuneration” and “authorized entity” limitations imported from the Chafee Amendment (17 U.S.C. § 121). The act of making copies of literary works in reliance on the Chafee Amendment is already subject to those conditions regardless of whether the circumvention of access controls is subject to the same conditions. Thus, duplicating those conditions in Section 1201 is not necessary, and will subject good faith accessibility efforts to increased legal risk.  2.  Device Unlocking: The Office Should Recommend A Permanent Exemption For All Devices. There is strong agreement among stakeholders and the public that “unlocking” consumer wireless devices in order to use them on different mobile networks should not be a violation of Section 1201. The Register has recommended, and the Librarian granted, exemptions for mobile phone unlocking in four consecutive triennial rulemakings. In the most recent rulemaking, the Register and the Librarian extended that exemption to all-purpose tablets, personal mobile connectivity devices, and wearable wireless devices. Both Congress and the Obama Administration have expressed their support for an unlocking exemption.5  A permanent exemption, however, should cover all devices that connect to cellular wireless networks.    First, prior triennial exemptions are an artificially narrow basis for any permanent exemption. By imposing onerous burdens of proof on proponents, the Office has made the exemption process expensive in terms of costs and resources. This, in turn, means that exemptions will be unfairly shaped by those can afford to ask for them and fail to capture all facets of the public interest. Congress can do better, and it should.   Second, more and more types of devices are being manufactured with cellular connectivity. Any permanent exemption that attempts to define categories of devices will inevitably fail to capture devices that become prominent in the market over the next three, six, or more years. The solution to this problem is a broad exemption that is flexible enough to protect users tomorrow as well as today.  Third, the DMCA was never intended to bolster the business models of wireless carriers, and it is not necessary for that purpose. No wireless carrier expressed a need for Section 1201 before it was enacted. Computer Programs: The Proposed Exemption For Non-Infringing Uses Is The Best Approach.  Many harmful effects of Section 1201 flow from its application to computer programs, including programs that are embedded in common products such as smartphones to medical devices to thermostats to vehicles. To an increasing degree, computer programs act on our behalf in the realms of communication, finance, transportation, health, work, and many others. The use of technological protection measures to control access to these programs, which trigger the application of Section 1201, means that the user of the program does not have full control over its operation. Often, the user (the owner or lawful possessor of the copy) cannot modify, audit, or even discover the complete functionality of the software to which they have entrusted their privacy and safety, without courting liability under Section 1201. And manufacturers have used the threat of 1201 liability to lock out competition, including competition in aftermarkets and from repaired or refurbished products, since its inception.  EFF commends the Copyright Office for recognizing the depth and seriousness of these problems. EFF strongly supports the Office’s proposal for “an exemption to explicitly permit circumvention undertaken to make non-infringing adaptations.”7 The benefit of that statutory language is that it links the application of Section 1201 directly to the well-developed limitations and defenses to copyright infringement, including fair use, without attempting to recapitulate or paraphrase those limitations within Section 1201, which would raise the possibility of inconsistent application. Also, by focusing on “non-infringing” conduct, this proposal would affirm that Section 1201 is tied to the purposes of copyright law and is not a computer trespass, business tort, or product safety statute.  This proposed permanent exemption will not cause significant “economically harmful unauthorized uses of copyrighted works,” particularly when weighed against the market-expanding competition and independent innovation that will result. Truly harmful activities will continue to be actionable as copyright infringement or under other law.  Diagnosis, maintenance, and repair of personal devices are important, and in need of greater legal certainty. There are, however, many other important and lawful reasons to modify copies of computer programs. Obsolete Technologies  EFF supports a permanent exemption that would permit circumvention to preserve the use of obsolete technologies. Many of the obsolete technologies that have been subject to temporary exemptions or proposed exemptions involve software as the copyrighted work to which access controls are applied. Thus, an exemption for non-infringing modification of computer programs would address many obsolete technologies as well.  5.  Reverse Engineering for Interoperability, Encryption Research, and Security Testing A. The Existing Exemptions Suffer From A Host of Flaws  The NOI identified a series of flaws in the existing exemptions for reverse engineering, but it is crucial for policymakers to be aware of several fundamental flaws not highlighted in the NOI, so they can avoid repeating those mistakes. These flaws are so serious that EFF is not aware of any case in which a permanent statutory exemption was held to protect an act of circumvention or trafficking.  As a general matter, the permanent statutory exemptions at issue require would-be beneficiaries to carefully adhere to strict technical requirements, then require them to come out ahead in a nebulous consideration of assorted factors. This combination of strict hurdles with ultimate uncertainty has ensured that the exemptions fail to protect the activities they were intended to defend.   More specifically, Section 1201(f) requires that a person’s “sole purpose” in circumventing be to enable a particular, narrow form of interoperability between computer programs. It prohibits a person from communicating the information they learn unless their “purpose” in doing so is “solely” to enable such interoperability. Non-infringing uses can easily run afoul of this “solely” restriction, because people generally have multiple purposes when trying to understand software and make it function in new ways.  Section 1201(f) also contains a nearly impenetrable definition of what it actually permits: “a person who has lawfully obtained the right to use a copy of a computer program may circumvent a technological measure that effectively controls access to a particular portion of that program for the sole purpose of identifying and analyzing those

[PAGE 6]
	   6 
elements of the program that are necessary to achieve interoperability of an independently created computer program with other programs” It is not clear why it matters which programs are to be made interoperable, or why one of the programs must be created “independently.” The exemption would be more clear if it simply permitted circumvention and trafficking in furtherance of noninfringing reverse engineering or interoperability activities.  Section 1201(g), the encryption research exemption, fails because it depends upon the application of nebulous factors that discriminate based upon the identity and communicative activities of the researcher. A researcher may be penalized under these factors if they publish information in a way that can be used by independent third parties to violate copyright or other laws, or if they fail to communicate with the copyright owner (which may not be the same entity as the one responsible for the integrity of the technological measure being researched). The factors also penalize a person for not being adequately educated or affiliated with an educational institution. The exemption also requires a researcher to seek permission before engaging in circumvention, which means the researcher must invite a potentially negative response from the manufacturer at an early stage, without being given any guarantee that their conduct will be considered lawful.  The encryption research exemption for 1201(a)(2) is limited to providing the technological means to another person with whom the researcher is working collaboratively. It does not accommodate other communications with students, vendors, academics, or the public.  Section 1201(j) again requires that a researcher act “solely” for a testing, investigating, or correction purpose. See Section 1201(j)(1), (4). The exemption’s factors again depend upon how the researcher communicates the information they learn and threaten liability based upon the actions of third parties outside of the researcher’s control. There is no dependable safe harbor for security testing.  For the foregoing reasons, the existing statutory exemptions are inadequate and inherently flawed. The Copyright Office’s recommendations should help policymakers learn from those mistakes.  B. The 2015 Rulemaking Exemption Should Not Be the Model for a Permanent Exemption  In the 2015 Rulemaking, the Copyright Office imposed requirements that re-create the fatal flaws of the statutory exemptions, adopted a narrow view of what research should be permissible, and imposed restrictions that are unrelated to the goals of copyright law.  First, the rulemaking exemption recreated the basic structure of strict technical requirements combined with vague restrictions. Research must be conducted “solely” for the listed purposes, and the acts of third parties outside of a researcher’s control may

[PAGE 7]
	   7 
disqualify the researcher from the exemption’s protection. As discussed above, “sole purpose” language threatens to disqualify researchers who have other valid purposes. And liability for acts of third parties should be handled under doctrines of secondary liability, not as redundant and vague restrictions on exemptions to Section 1201.  Second, the rulemaking exemption unwisely limits security research to consumer devices. Researchers such as Dr. Matthew Green seek to evaluate and ensure the security of devices such as encryption modules for financial transactions like ATM withdrawals, toll collection systems, and other infrastructure that communicates with computerized vehicles. A researcher who lawfully obtains such a device should be able to audit and improve its security, and to share their findings and methods.  Third, exemptions should not restrict lawful activity based upon considerations that have nothing to do with copyright law. Limitations in the rulemaking exemption mean that liability under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act depends upon whether a person creates potential safety hazards, even if they do not rise to the level that would otherwise be actionable under laws designed to address personal injury. Moreover, the person with a cause of action is not the person whose safety is hypothetically threatened, but a copyright owner who suffers no injury. Section 1201 provides a powerful and dangerous tool for the government and private rightsholders, and should not be used to create duplicative or expanded liability for acts governed by other areas of law.  Fourth, as described above with regard to wireless device unlocking, the onerous and artificial burdens of proof imposed on the triennial rulemaking process make the outcomes of that process a poor starting point for permanent exemptions.  A superior approach would be a simple and clear exemption for circumvention and trafficking in furtherance of noninfringing security research.  6.  Congress and the Copyright Office Each Should Take Steps To Protect Third-Party Assistance With Circumvention The Copyright Office can protect some forms of third-party assistance without waiting for Congressional action via rulemaking exemptions to Section 1201(a)(1) liability. Sections 1201(a)(2) and (b)(1) do not ban all circumvention technologies and services. A technology or service must fall into one of the three enumerated categories: being “primarily designed or produced” for circumvention, having only “limited commercially significant purpose or use other than” circumvention, or being “marketed” for circumvention.   Even under the most expansive reading of these prohibitions, technologies or services that incidentally involve circumvention are permissible. For example, an auto mechanic may offer a repair service. Most of the time, circumvention is not necessary to achieve the repair, but sometimes it is. Customers are attracted to the service because their car will work again, not because they want access to copyrighted automobile software. The customer may never have access to the software at all.

[PAGE 8]
	   8 
However, the mechanic is likely subject to the prohibitions of 1201(a)(1), because they do engage in an act of circumvention. If the Librarian had issued an exemption to 1201(a)(1) allowing third-party assistance for auto repair, then mechanics could offer the range of services permitted by the anti-trafficking provisions.   While the Copyright Office and the Librarian cannot issue exemptions to 1201(a)(2) or 1201(b) liability, the Librarian can grant exemptions to 1201(a)(1) liability that contemplate third-party assistance. As discussed, there will be types of assistance and services that benefit from the exemption without rising to the level of a trafficking violation, and extending the rulemaking exemption to then will help the public make effective use of the rights protected in the rulemaking.   This is consistent with Congress’s intent. Part of the essential bargain of Section 1201 was that technologies and services with substantial non-infringing uses were to remain lawful. See 144 Cong. Rec. S4884-01 (1998) at S4890: (Senator Ashcroft: “I could agree to support the bill’s approach of outlawing certain devices because I was repeatedly assured that the device prohibitions in 1201(a)(2) and 1201(b) are aimed at so-called ‘black boxes’ and not at legitimate consumer electronics and computer products that have substantial non-infringing uses. I specifically worked for and achieved changes to the bill to make sure that no court would misinterpret this bill as outlawing legitimate consumer electronics devices or computer hardware. As a result, neither section 1201(a)(2) nor section 1201(b) should be read as outlawing any device with substantial non-infringing uses, as per the tests provided in those sections.”).  EFF agrees that greater clarity is necessary to encourage lawful third-party assistance under Section 1201. At present, the law impermissibly deters third-party assistance and technologies that do not infringe copyright (directly or contributorily). Congress could improve the law by making clear that the ban does not extend to such services and technologies.  Conclusion EFF thanks the Copyright Office for exploring beneficial changes to Section 1201. That law is in need of reform to cure its Constitutional and practical defects. We urge the Office to recommend the Unlocking Technology Act of 2015, or other permanent exemptions that apply broadly to non-infringing activities, without complex or subjective qualifications that, as history has shown, render those exemptions ineffective.
