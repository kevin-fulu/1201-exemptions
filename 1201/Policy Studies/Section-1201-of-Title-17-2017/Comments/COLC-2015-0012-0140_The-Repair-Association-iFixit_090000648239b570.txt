Introduction
About The Repair Association 
The Repair Association, formerly known as the Digital Right to Repair Coalition, is a 
501(c)6 trade association founded in July of 2013 to support individuals and businesses 
engaged in repair, reuse, and recycling of digital electronic parts and products. Our 
members range in size from individual hobbyists to multinational repair and services 
providers, and include consumer rights organizations, sustainability advocates, 
agricultural organizations, flexible labor networks, equipment trading networks, and 
logistical services networks.
The Repair Association has advised state governments on right to repair laws based on 
the 2012 Massachusetts Automotive Right to Repair law.1 This bill provided a clear path 
for state legislative action, and also served as the foundation of major national voluntary 
agreements adopted by automotive2 and commercial vehicle3 manufacturers. The 
success of this approach strongly suggests that commercial interests and consumer 
repair are complementary values that can both be served by well-considered policy.
About iFixit
iFixit.com is an international, open-source, online repair manual for everything. Our 
mission is to provide people with the knowledge they need to make their things work for 
as long as possible.
iFixit represents a global community of makers, tinkerers, fixers, and repair 
professionals. In 2015, the iFixit community taught repair to over 80 million people from
almost every country in the world. The strongly collaborative group has published over 
20,000 repair guides. This massive, free resource has helped people fix everything from 
cellphones to game consoles, toys to musical instruments. iFixit also stands firm in its

Reply
The Repair Association and iFixit thank the Copyright Office for this opportunity to 
reply to the comments of the Association of Equipment Manufacturers (AEM) and the 
Equipment Dealers Association (EDA).4 
The market in “commercially available” software
In their filing, the AEM and EDA argue against exemptions on a broad class of software, 
saying that:
“AEM and EDA caution against enactment of any permanent exemption where 
the copyrighted work, such as vehicle software or software updates, are 
commercially available products or services. If the works are already 
commercially available, the proposed permanent exemption would diminish or 
destroy the value of the vehicle software or software updates and unfairly disrupt
the competitive landscape for both AEM and EDA members.” (p. 2)
This argument, and particularly these examples, presumes that software integrated into 
equipment is “commercially available to consumers.” This is not, and has never been, 
the case. Instead, this sort of software (often called embedded software or firmware) is 
included with the product. This is not surprising, since if it was not included, or was 
sold for an additional fee, the product would not work! As a result, vehicle software and 
software updates are not typically available separate from the hardware they power. This 
is particularly true in a world where, as we noted in our original filing, software is “eating
the world” and therefore is included in essentially every product and category of 
product.
As a result, it is not surprising that there is no public market in firmware distinct from 
the market in the underlying products — you can’t browse to Amazon’s “firmware” 
section, or find a firmware aisle at Best Buy. Instead, the software produced by AEM’s 
members is typically offered free of charge to consumers. For example, one can visit the

Intel website and download firmware for any of its products at no charge.5 Similarly, 
Bosch is the leading manufacturer of Engine Control Units that are used within most 
motor vehicles worldwide. Bosch makes their firmware publicly available.6 
Because of this availability, owners that need to return firmware to the original settings, 
or obtain updates, can do so easily, without any economic harm to manufacturers like 
Bosch, their retail distributors, or to future owners. The same type of firmware access is 
common for thousands of other components and presents no economic risk, nor any risk
of piracy, to manufacturers. There is no economic value to firmware outside of the 
unique part and no value to preventing restoration or use.
This economic model for the production of firmware predates the DMCA, and will 
survive any broadened exceptions. First, as already noted, the economic driver for 
manufacturers is the sale of hardware, not software. This effect is strengthened by the 
nature of firmware, which is specific to particular pieces of hardware. Bosch ECU 
firmware, for example, will not work on ECUs made by other manufacturers, which both
incentivizes Bosch to produce new firmware for new hardware, and essentially 
eliminates the supposed market discussed by AEM’s comments. In addition, no-cost 
distribution of updated firmware is a form of safety and security defect support, and so 
this software will continue to be produced for those reasons – no additional protection, 
above and beyond that already offered by copyright law, is needed. 
The real market in this area is not in selling software, but rather in going beyond 
software to limit competition in the market for repair, and to allow consumer-hostile 
behaviors that break devices owned by consumers without the consent or participation 
of their owners. For example, Apple recently shipped an iOS “update” that broke the 
iPhone for hundreds of thousands of users who had done nothing more sinister than 
replace broken physical screens.7 Similarly, a recent update to HP printers prevented 
them from operating with ink bought by a vendor other than HP.8 These “updates” 
suggest that AEM’s interest is not in fostering a market for firmware (which would be

Public safety and the environment
Under the logic of the AEM/EDA response, copyright law should be used to regulate 
and enforce matters of public safety, environment and economic policy. In their 
response, as well as submissions to past triennial DMCA rulemakings, AEM/EDA 
propose that employees of manufacturers and dealerships help enforce federal policies 
by refusing to allow competition for repair. As AEM/EDA’s comments read:
“… the restriction of this information to a finite number of highly qualified and 
readily identifiable parties aids in the efficient and effective enforcement of safety and 
environmental regulations.” (emphasis added) (p. 3)
When making this argument, AEM/EDA fail to cite to relevant statutory language, 
because there is none. But pursuing this route would not just be a violation of clear 
statutory and Congressional intent. It would also take the Office outside of its core 
competencies, inevitably creating inefficiencies both for the Office and for the newly 
regulated industries that would come under its purview. 
The Copyright Office should reject this interpretation of its role, and leave the 
enforcement of safety and environmental regulations to the agencies best situated to do 
so.
“Economic disadvantage”
AEM/EDA’s comments suggest that any exceptions to the DMCA will enable and 
encourage repair businesses to make unsafe modifications to firmware, and put “safe” 
providers at an economic disadvantage. (p. 1)
This position ignores the existence of a mass of federal, state, and local statutes and 
regulations that deal with safety and other issues. These rules have been created through
the deliberate action of appropriate legislative, administrative, and judicial bodies. 
Refocusing enforcement of the Copyright Act on copyright concerns will not impact or 
lessen the application of those laws and regulations.

The use of electronic locks that prevent repair, or use of consumables such as printer 
ink9, are rarely, if ever, been about safety – instead, they have been about protecting the 
competitive position of manufacturers for repair services or consumable purchases.   
The “economic disadvantage” that AEM/EDA seeks to avoid is that of competition for 
goods and services provided by those who would seek to repair and improve the original 
products. 
Where hardware manufacturers are actually providing firmware that meets the needs of 
consumers, new entrants will have no “unfair” advantage. Instead, limited by the same 
safety and environmental regulations as the original manufacturers, they will advance 
the “useful arts” through the basic rights of fair use, first sale, interoperability, and 
repair – rights that a broad interpretation of Section 1201 has taken away from them. 
Conclusion
As software has come to be embedded in everything, Section 1201 has come to have a 
direct bearing on the right of every American, including both consumers and businesses,
to repair and modify the devices they have purchased. The current review process has 
served mostly to show that existing exemptions, and the exemption process, are broken. 
For Americans to regain the right to repair their devices, they must be broadly reformed.
