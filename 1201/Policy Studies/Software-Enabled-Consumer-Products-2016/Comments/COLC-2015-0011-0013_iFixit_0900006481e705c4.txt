Interests of iFixit   iFixit is the international, open-source, online repair manual for everything. The organization is devoted to making “repair accessible and easy for as many people around the world as possible.”1 Repair saves consumers money and protects the environment. As a trade, repair also represents more than 3 million domestic jobs.  iFixit represents a global community of makers, hardware hackers, fixers, tinkerers, and repair professionals. The iFixit community is over one million people strong—and the collaborative group has published nearly 20,000 repair guides on iFixit.com. In 2015, this massive, free resource has helped tens of millions of people around the globe fix everything from mobile phones to game consoles, toys to musical instruments. As a founding member of The Repair Association, iFixit also stands firm in its support of tinkerers and independent repair professionals.    Introduction   The gradual and now ubiquitous integration of software into consumer products has challenged the ability of the current copyright regime to address increasingly complex issues of ownership, access, and authorization.2 Every product—from primitive to cutting-edge—eventually breaks. But most products can also be repaired.

Cars are an important example. When a car’s maintenance light flashes on its dashboard, traditionally, the owner could open the hood and search for the problem. Owners could make repairs for themselves, if they were sophisticated enough—perhaps consulting a manual to guide the process or identify replacement parts. Failing that, the owner could also shop around for a mechanic (either with a dealership or from an independent repair shop), receive quotes and estimates, and pay a mechanic to repair what is broken. Copyright law has stymied this traditional model of self-reliance and market competition for repairs—in ways Congress and the Constitution3 did not intend.   It's been years since cars were mere mechanical contrivances. A modern car is equipped with dozens of computers running hundreds of thousands of lines of computer code. Because software and firmware are copyrightable, manufacturers can assert unprecedented levels of control over the aftermarket and repair of vehicles. If only dealerships have the ability and the tools necessary to access, modify, and understand that code, then only manufacturers will be able to make repairs necessary for complex, modern vehicles. This problem is not limited to vehicles; all software-enabled products suffer from analogous issues, and this has a profound and negative effect on consumers and repair professions. Recently, a catastrophic software error in Apple’s iPhones has inspired the ire of millions of Apple users, because it is systematically limiting users’ ability to repair their iPhones. Indeed, there are countless examples6 of the strange interactions between the law’s agenda for protecting software copyrights and unintended consequences for hardware and consumers. This all raises the question: Should copyright law permit this?  The ability for the public to repair its products promotes creativity and reduces ecological and economic waste. Although repair has historically been reasonably permissible or shielded by legal principles such as fair use7 or the first sale doctrine,8 the introduction of software has frustrated this. iFixit proposes that copyright law expand its exemptions to include repairing software-enabled devices, thus returning the right to repair back to the consumer as well as independent repair shops. The Current Copyright Regime Unreasonably Restricts The Public’s Ability to Repair Software-Enabled Devices   iFixit is primarily concerned with the public’s ability to access and repair the hardware they have bought—hardware they believe they own. Software is always installed on, and as a result inextricably a part of, the hardware that contains it. When it comes to embedded software, the hardware is useless without the software. Repairs to latter affect the functionality of the former. That is to say that many questions regarding the laws and regulations of software also regard its corresponding hardware.    As digital intellectual property issues began to boil over into the physical world, Congress passed the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (“DMCA”),12 to address rightsholders’ concerns. The radical overhaul of the domestic copyright system accomplished its goals of providing greater protection to rightsholders in the digital era—but this came at the expense of shifting copyrights carefully weighed balance in favor of rightsholders at the expense of consumers.13 Critically, the DMCA’s safeguards were put in place to prevent the copying of entertainment media, such as                                                 11  Jim Lynch, Snapshot of Worldwide Electronics Recycling 2013, IFIXIT (May 7, 2013), http://ifixit.org/blog/4662/snapshot-of-worldwide-electronics-recycling-2013/. 12  Pub. In terms of the repair market, manufacturers have wielded the DMCA to establish a repair monopoly over physical products with essential software.  An increasingly commonplace trend in copyright and anti-circumvention threats involves manufacturers bootstrapping the rights afforded to software to impose restrictions on the use of its (generally non-copyrightable) hardware.14  Courts have broadly accepted licenses and contract provisions that further limit consumer’s ability to access hardware.15  Section 1201(c)3 of the DMCA attempts to limit the statute's scope solely to protecting rightholders’ interests in software and attempts to avoid interfering with hardware.16 However, this section largely expresses Congress’s intent to not proscribe, prescribe, or privilege any specific technological protection measure or circumventing device. This provision does not go far enough to preventing software’s overflow into hardware.

The Notice of Inquiry also illuminates how Congress took great care to ensure software repair was balanced in the new laws, but there is a conspicuous absence of discussion on the effects on hardware. Congress revisited the issues surrounding software and copyright law with the DMCA. As particularly relevant here, the DMCA amended section 117 of the Copyright Act to permit the reproduction of computer programs for the purposes of machine maintenance or repair following a court of appeals decision that cast doubt on the ability of independent service organizations to repair computer hardware. This provision foreshadows the more general concerns raised by the spread of software in everyday products—namely, that maintaining or repairing a software-enabled product often will require copying of the software.17   Since software is a protected work under copyright law, Congress and the courts have been careful to carve out certain protections for downstream users of software.18 After Mai Industries, practitioners and commentators feared that the Ninth Circuit’s onerous interpretation of software infringement would limit computer repair. This so concerned Congress, it enacted section 117(c) in the DMCA to lift the restriction on software use. The DMCA’s amendments, although addressing issues at the software level, failed to effectively address similar repair issues the spilled over into the hardware.    Copyright Law Should Be Amended to Safeguard the Public’s Ability to Repair Its Software-Enabled Devices    Repair benefits everyone. Unfortunately, the collateral damage of copyright law’s overreach in the digital world stymies the public’s ability make repairs.
