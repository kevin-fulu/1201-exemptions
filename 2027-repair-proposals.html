2027 Repair Proposals — DMCA §1201 New-Class Filings

Forward-looking petition plan for the 10th triennial anticircumvention rulemaking (NPRM mid-2026, Final Rule ~Oct 2027).

Cycle10th triennial
NPRMExpected Jun–Jul 2026
Final RuleExpected ~Oct 2027
StatusPlanning — pre-coalition signoff

This page formalizes a five-new-class petition strategy for the 2027 cycle, plus streamlined renewals of every 2024 grant. Each filing is structured around a cohesive user community and an evidence record modeled on the closest 2024-granted class — the Office's own pattern.

The 2024 Register denied "commercial and industrial equipment" broadly (Proposed Class 5, broader scope) for the same reason it denied Proposed Class 12 in 2021: stated as "insufficient record + non-cohesive class." Read the Office's grant pattern across cycles and the actual decision variable is record depth: the "non-cohesive class" framing is largely pretext used to deny when the record is thin. Wins in 2024 — food-prep (b)(16), medical-device (b)(17), vehicle/vessel data (b)(14) — all turned on concrete, named, documented use cases combined with a tight copyright posture. Our work-around: (a) split the rulemaking record into cohesive user communities (not equipment categories), (b) develop the record before filing, not after, and (c) use specific equipment examples to build per-category record depth — the diversity of examples is the argument for the class's breadth, not an argument against it. The 2017 §1201 Study itself (PDF p. 94) "recommends against limiting an exemption to specific technologies or devices."

Full strategy document with witness lists and class-language drafts: 1201/Claude Reports/2026-05-23__2027-Cycle-Multi-Class-Petition-Plan.md. Citations to the Register's reports keyed to 1201/Claude Reports/2026-05-24__Office-Policy-Reports-and-2027-Plan-Implications.md.

The Office's own position on repair

The Register has already endorsed the substance of what we're petitioning for. The 2017 §1201 study (Section 1201 of Title 17 — A Report of the Register of Copyrights) recommended to Congress a permanent statutory exemption for diagnosis, maintenance, and repair under §1201(a). The 2016 software-products study (Software-Enabled Consumer Products) found that §117 of the Copyright Act and existing doctrines (fair use, idea/expression, merger) already cover the underlying copyright activity in repair. Together the reports give us four citations that pre-rebut the most common opposition arguments:

1. The Register supports a permanent repair exemption. "A limited and properly‐tailored permanent exemption for [diagnosis, repair, and maintenance], including circumventing obsolete access controls for continued functioning of a device, would be consistent with the statute's overall policy goals." — 2017 §1201 Study, PDF p. 90. The Office said the rulemaking is a "somewhat ill‐fitting regulatory approach" (p. 92) for cross-cutting repair issues. Our triennial filings are interim relief while Congress catches up.

2. The Office expressly recommends against device-specific limitations. "The Office recommends against limiting an exemption to specific technologies or devices, such as motor vehicles, as any statutory language would likely be soon outpaced by technology." — 2017 §1201 Study, PDF p. 94. This is the Office's own position that broader, cross-device repair exemptions are preferable. Cite it whenever opposition argues a class is "too broad."

3. Safety concerns belong outside copyright. "To the extent that commenters oppose an exemption for repair out of non‐copyright related concerns, such as public safety, the Office believes these matters are better addressed through laws or regulations outside of the Copyright Act." — 2017 §1201 Study, PDF p. 94. The Office's own framing for our Filing 5 safety carve-outs.

4. §117(d) is the Register's preferred statutory anchor for "maintenance" and "repair." The Office recommended that any repair exemption use §117(d)'s definitions: "the 'maintenance' of a machine is the servicing of the machine in order to make it work in accordance with its original specifications" and "the 'repair' of a machine is the restoring of the machine to the state of working in accordance with its original specifications." — 17 U.S.C. §117(d); 2017 §1201 Study, PDF p. 94. Our class-language drafts should mirror this. The "original specifications" framing also pre-rebuts the modification / tinkering objection: we are not asking to enhance or customize devices, only restore them.

What's NOT in our favor: the Office rejected a §1201(a) infringement-nexus requirement (would weaken copyright owners' control over online access to creative works, 2017 Study pp. 42–46) and rejected categorical exclusion of "device- or machine-enabling computer programs" (line-drawing problems, p. 47). Our argument cannot be "TPMs on repair targets are categorically outside §1201"; it must be "these TPMs are non-creative-works gating and the Office has endorsed the repair-exemption framework."

The five proposed filings

Filing 1 · Agricultural & Construction Equipment

Confidence: High

Extends the (b)(13) motorized-land-vehicle pattern to (a) stationary agricultural systems and (b) construction-equipment clarification.

Scope
  • Stationary ag: irrigation controllers, grain dryers, milking robots, barn/livestock automation, precision-ag base stations, automated-greenhouse controllers, seed conditioning.
  • Stationary construction processing: asphalt & concrete batch plants, aggregate processing, fixed crushing/screening.
  • Mobile construction (clarifying ask): confirm that (b)(13) "motorized land vehicle" reaches excavators, loaders, dozers, mobile pavers.
Precedent
2024 §201.40(b)(13) motorized land vehicle (repair-class template); (b)(14) vehicle/vessel data (data-access analog if needed).
Coalition lead
Repair Association · co-leads AFBF (Farm Bureau), AED (construction).
Risk
Low. Strongest filing on the list. Stationary-ag is a clean gap in the existing vehicle class; the construction-clarification angle is the Office's preferred posture (clarify rather than re-codify).
Expected outcome
New (b)(N) class covering stationary ag + stationary construction processing; on-record clarification that (b)(13) reaches mobile construction.

Filing 2 · Scientific & Laboratory Equipment

Confidence: Very high

Repair of software-enabled scientific and laboratory instruments — every category of research instrument, from mass spectrometers to electron microscopes to sequencers. The 2024 Class 5 proponents (Public Knowledge & iFixit Initial Comment, PDF p. 9) explicitly excluded "devices designed or marketed for medical, scientific, or consumer use" from their petition — meaning the §1201 record on scientific equipment is clean and unopposed. This filing closes that deliberate gap with a comprehensive record across the full surface of research instrumentation.

Why now
Two things make this the cleanest filing in the 2027 portfolio. One: the 2024 deliberate carve-out (above) — the Office has never said "no" to a scientific-instrument repair class because no one has ever asked. Already shown as a gap row in the repair diagram. Two: the 2017 §1201 Study formally recommended Congress adopt a permanent statutory repair exemption (PDF pp. 88–96); the Office's own analytical position supports the filing. The scientific user community (research universities, biotech, pharma, ISOs) is highly organized and budget-constrained — perfect record-building conditions.
Example instruments (record depth)
  • Mass spectrometers and analytical chains (HPLC, UPLC, GC, GC-MS, LC-MS) — Thermo Fisher, Agilent, Waters, Bruker, Sciex.
  • Genomic / proteomic sequencers — Illumina, PacBio, Oxford Nanopore.
  • Electron microscopes (TEM, SEM, FIB, cryo-EM); confocal and super-resolution optical microscopes — JEOL, Hitachi, Thermo Fisher, Zeiss, Leica.
  • Flow cytometers and cell sorters — BD, Beckman, Cytek.
  • Spectrophotometers (UV-Vis, FTIR, NMR), thermal cyclers / qPCR machines, ultracentrifuges, plate readers.
  • Calibration, metrology, and electronic test & measurement equipment.

As with every filing in this portfolio: these are examples that build the per-category record. The class itself is scientific and laboratory equipment broadly — naming specific instrument types is record depth, not a list of separate asks.

Copyright posture
The TPMs at issue (service-mode passwords, OEM calibration software, authenticated parts, encrypted firmware, phone-home license checks) gate the instrument's device-control firmware — they do not gate access to copyrighted creative works. The 2016 Software Study found that "repair supports — rather than displaces — the purpose of the embedded programs that control that device" (PDF p. 40) and that §117(a) "should adequately protect most repair and maintenance activities" (p. 35). The 2017 §1201 Study went further and recommended Congress adopt a permanent statutory repair exemption using §117(d)'s "maintenance" and "repair" definitions. Even the joint opposition coalition (AAP, ESA, MPAA & RIAA) conceded in the 2016 record that "automobile repair or other forms of repair might present a unique and distinguishable set of circumstances" (2017 Study at p. 93 n.508). Scientific instruments fit that distinguishable-set framing exactly.
Precedent
2024 §201.40(b)(17) medical-device repair — closest pattern: same ISO-repair industry structure, same "related data files" issue (calibration files, run-method files), same professional/institutional user posture. (b)(16) retail commercial food-prep — analogous "institutional commercial user" template.
Coalition lead
Public Knowledge + Repair Association.
Co-leads
University procurement consortia (APLU / AAU member institutions); scientific-instrument ISO industry trade groups; research-society instrumentation interests (ACS analytical division; ASMS for mass-spec; ASCB / similar for microscopy); SERI (sustainability — abandoned-instrument e-waste).
Record-building plan
Service-contract cost differentials between OEM and independent ISOs (commonly 2–3× for the same scope); documented refusal-to-service by OEMs after end-of-life on instruments still in research use; downtime-cost impact on grant-funded research — NIH / NSF / DOE cycle delays, missed publication deadlines, lost graduate-student productivity; core-facility utilization records from research universities showing instrument-availability shortfalls; OEM parts-availability gaps for instruments >7 years old; "phone-home" authentication that bricks instruments without a manufacturer license check (specific named instrument lines).
Witnesses
2+ university core-facility directors (mass-spec, sequencing, and EM cores at separate institutions); 1+ biotech / pharma lab manager; 1+ scientific-instrument ISO repair company; 1+ NIH- or NSF-funded PI documenting research delay from equipment lockouts.
Risk
Low. Direct analog of the 2024 medical-device grant. Clean §1201 record (no prior denial); no organized opposition (the deliberate 2024 carve-out means the usual rightsholder coalition has not staked out a position). The strategic variable here, as in every filing, is record depth — and the user community is unusually well-organized for record-building.
Expected outcome
New (b)(N) for scientific and laboratory equipment repair, with related-data-files coverage (calibration files, run-method files) on the (b)(17) model.

Filing 3 · American Re-industrialization: Manufacturing Equipment (rebranded + selectively expanded)

Confidence: Medium-low

Anchored to the post-CHIPS-Act political moment: domestic factory owners need autonomy over their own operations to ramp up American manufacturing. Covers the full envelope of equipment a U.S. factory needs to operate — production lines, factory-floor automation, fab tooling, and manufacturing-facility infrastructure. Same substantive ask as the post-2024 industrial-automation strategy, reframed around reshoring and operator sovereignty, with selective scope expansion into adjacent fab infrastructure.

Political frame
Reshoring, domestic manufacturing autonomy, and factory-operator sovereignty over their own equipment. Anchors to the CHIPS Act, the IRA's domestic-content provisions, and the bipartisan re-industrialization consensus. Reframes a twice-denied "industrial repair" ask into an "American competitiveness" ask — without changing the underlying copyright posture.
Scope (4 sub-asks)
  • 3a · Regulated production lines. PLCs and process controllers governing food / beverage / pharma manufacturing subject to FDA / FSMA documentation requirements. Strongest harm story: recall-traceability and audit requirements compound the lock-in cost.
  • 3b · Abandoned-vendor / end-of-life industrial controllers. Explicit class for equipment whose OEM no longer supports the product. Strongest copyright posture — vendor has exited the market.
  • 3c · Factory-floor automation. Mid-sized factory robotics and motion controllers; industrial 3D printers (Stratasys, EOS, Markforged, HP); semiconductor-fab process tools; industrial test & measurement equipment used for production QA.
  • 3d · Manufacturing facility infrastructure. Industrial-scale HVAC for factory production environments, cleanroom HVAC and control systems, factory-scale power management. Distinguished from Filing 5's commercial HVAC by user (factory operator vs. building owner) and purpose (production-environment process control vs. occupant comfort).
Precedent
2024 §201.40(b)(17) medical devices (professional / institutional repair under firmware control); (b)(21) 3D-printer feedstock (adjacent, does not reach device repair). Adverse: 2021 Proposed Class 12 + 2024 Proposed Class 5 (broader scope) denials — the re-industrialization framing is the strategic response to those denials.
Coalition lead
Repair Association industrial track + Reshoring Initiative.
Co-leads
SMRP (Maintenance & Reliability Professionals); AMUG (additive manufacturing); manufacturing-policy think tanks aligned with the post-CHIPS-Act agenda; mid-sized manufacturer associations (SME, NTMA); semiconductor-equipment user groups for the fab-tool sub-leg.
Expected opposition
PMMI, NAM, and individual OEMs (Rockwell, Siemens-aligned voices, Stratasys, ABB, Fanuc, Applied Materials, Lam Research). The reshoring framing may split NAM internally — large multinational OEMs vs. domestic mid-sized manufacturers who are NAM's growing constituency.
Risk
Medium-high. Office has denied this bucket twice; the re-industrialization framing is the strategic response, not a guarantee. Four sub-asks give multiple shots. 3b (abandoned vendor) remains the most likely to win because copyright concern is weakest when the vendor has exited. 3d (facility infrastructure) is the most novel and most opposed sub-ask.
Expected outcome
1–2 of 4 sub-asks granted (most likely 3b abandoned-vendor and either 3a regulated-pharma or 3c factory-floor); the others build the 2030 record. The political frame may produce sympathetic Register dicta even where the scope is denied — useful for the 2030 cycle.

Filing 4 · General-Purpose Computing & Networking (broadened to include commercial-use PCs, Macs, workstations)

Confidence: Medium-low (full) / Medium-high (commercial-PC + workstation carve-out)

Hardware repair under firmware lockout across the full general-purpose computing stack — from consumer-grade laptops used commercially through professional workstations through datacenter hardware. The (b)(15) consumer-device class's "primarily designed for use by consumers" gate is genuinely ambiguous for PCs / Macs / laptops used in commercial settings — this filing closes that gap explicitly, and folds enterprise IT in alongside it under a unified "hardware repair, not software access" framing.

Scope — general-purpose computing
  • Consumer-grade computers in commercial use — laptops, desktops, all-in-ones used by businesses, schools, healthcare practices, government agencies, nonprofits.
  • Professional workstations — Apple Mac Pro / Mac Studio, Dell Precision, HP Z-series, Lenovo ThinkStation. Clearly outside (b)(15)'s "consumer" gate by design and marketing.
  • Apple Silicon parts-pairing across the Mac line — T2 / Secure Enclave authentication; locked SSDs and Touch-ID parts; soldered RAM with brick-on-replacement behavior.
  • ChromeOS and Windows-Hello hardware bindings; TPM-anchored boot lockouts after motherboard replacement.
Scope — networking & datacenter
  • Servers and server boards (CPU, memory, storage, NIC).
  • Network switches, routers, optical / transport gear (port-level component, fan, PSU swap).
  • Storage arrays (SSD / HDD, controller card).
  • UPS / PDU / datacenter power (battery, board, monitoring card).
  • Datacenter environmental units (CRAC / CRAH controllers, in-row cooling).
Explicit exclusions
  • Bypassing software entitlements / per-port / per-VM / capacity licensing.
  • Modifying or accessing OS, hypervisor, or management plane beyond what is strictly necessary for hardware component substitution.
  • Defeating subscription gates on software features or DRM-protected content (FairPlay, Widevine, streaming-service DRM).

Why these exclusions are doctrinally important. The 2016 Software Study draws a distinction we explicitly track: embedded software that is "invisible to the consumer" (mechanical controllers, peripheral firmware, parts-pairing chips) is owned for §117(a) purposes, but software that is "a more significant element of the transaction" (an OS like macOS or Windows) is more likely treated as licensed (PDF p. 35–36). Our scope sits squarely on the owned-firmware side of that line — we're asking to circumvent component-authentication chips so the user can replace failed hardware, not to access or modify the OS. The exclusions above make that distinction explicit so opposition cannot conflate the two.

Why now (commercial PC)
(b)(15) reaches devices "primarily designed for use by consumers." Commercial-use PCs and workstations sit outside that gate by both design intent and marketing. The 2012 SFLC PC-repair denial is 13 years old; the evidentiary landscape has changed completely (Apple Silicon parts-pairing, locked SSDs, T2 / secure-enclave authentication, surface bonding). A direct ask for general-purpose computing in commercial use rests on a much stronger record than 2012's, and on a class-definition theory that does not exist in any current exemption.
Why now (enterprise IT)
HPE / Dell / Cisco lockouts on EOL hardware; license-keyed feature unlocks on storage arrays; PSU and optic authentication on network switches. Refurbisher-industry economic data is now available at scale (SERI, ITRH) — the sustainability and e-waste-diversion story is concrete in a way it wasn't in prior cycles.
Precedent
2024 §201.40(b)(15) consumer-device repair (analogous for non-commercial PCs; this filing extends the same logic to commercial use); (b)(17) medical devices ("professional repair under firmware control, including related data files" template for the enterprise IT leg).
Coalition lead
Repair Association IT / PC track + SERI (sustainability) or ITRH (IT-refurbisher industry).
Co-leads
EFF; nonprofit IT-refurbisher consortia; commercial-PC user communities (school IT, small-business IT, nonprofit / philanthropy IT); MSPs and managed-IT trade associations.
Expected opposition
Apple (most aggressive — has fought repair exemptions across multiple cycles), Microsoft, HPE, Dell, Cisco, NetApp, Lenovo. Strongest opposition of any filing.
Risk
High for full scope. Medium for a commercial-PC + workstation carve-out (clearly outside (b)(15)'s consumer gate). The narrow framing — hardware repair only, no licensing / OS access / content-DRM bypass — is essential.
Expected outcome
Likely partial grant. Strongest case: professional workstations (Mac Pro, Dell Precision, HP Z) — clearly commercial by design. Medium case: commercial-use laptops / desktops (Office may say (b)(15) already covers and decline to extend). Datacenter / enterprise gear: likely denied at full scope; narrow abandoned-vendor IT carve-out plausible (parallel to Filing 3b).

Filing 5 · Commercial Premises Equipment

Confidence: Medium-high

One broad class covering software-enabled equipment installed on commercial, institutional, and multi-family premises that uses TPMs to gate physical repair. The diversity of equipment categories below is not an argument for separate filings — it is the evidence that this is a single systemic problem affecting one coherent user community (operators of commercial premises). The breadth of the class follows from the breadth of the record.

Why one broad class
A property owner faces the same §1201 problem whether the locked equipment is the building's HVAC, the laundry room's washing machines, the parking lot's EV charger, the basement's solar inverter, the lobby's elevator-access controller, or the gym's connected treadmill. Same TPM patterns (service-mode passwords, parts-pairing, OEM-only software, phone-home authentication). Same harm pattern (operator can't repair what they own; OEM monopoly drives up service cost; abandoned-vendor equipment becomes unrepairable e-waste). Same copyright posture (firmware gating device function, not gating access to creative works). Splitting the class by equipment category would mis-frame a systemic issue as a series of niche concerns. The 2017 §1201 Study (PDF p. 94) puts this point explicitly: the Office "recommends against limiting an exemption to specific technologies or devices, such as motor vehicles, as any statutory language would likely be soon outpaced by technology."
Example equipment (record-depth, not separate asks)
  • Commercial HVAC — rooftop units, chillers, VRF systems, walk-in refrigeration, commercial-scale heating/cooling. Heavy lockouts (Carrier, Trane, Daikin, Honeywell, Johnson Controls service-tech-only software). Excludes Filing 3's industrial-scale factory HVAC and the residential HVAC already reached by (b)(15)'s home-systems leg.
  • Building management systems (BMS) and building automation controllers (Niagara, Tridium, etc.) — heavy service-mode lockouts.
  • Commercial elevator and access controllers (with life-safety carve-out below).
  • Solar inverters and microinverter systems — Enphase, SolarEdge, SMA, Fronius, GoodWe. Installer-only software gates everything from a fault-reset to a board replacement.
  • Commercial battery energy-storage systems — Tesla Powerwall through Megapack scale, LG, Enphase IQ, EG4.
  • Energy management systems (EMS) and microgrid controllers.
  • EV charging infrastructure — Level 2 and DC fast chargers in commercial, fleet, multi-family, and workplace settings (ChargePoint, EVgo, Blink, Tesla). Documented network-collapse bricking incidents.
  • Commercial laundry — Alliance/Speed Queen, Continental, Dexter, Maytag Commercial; service-mode lockouts documented in laundromat and hospitality-laundry operations.
  • Commercial dishwashing equipment (Hobart service codes) not in (b)(16)'s food-prep class — institutional kitchens, hotels, factories.
  • Commercial espresso, garment-care, minibar, and vending equipment — brand-dependent service modes; record-depth varies but contributes to the breadth-of-pattern argument.
  • Commercial fitness and wellness equipment — Life Fitness, Precor, Technogym, Matrix, Cybex. Diagnostic service modes; lighter parts-pairing than the heavy-lockout categories above. Included because including it costs us nothing and helps establish the equipment-breadth pattern.
  • Commercial cleaning equipment — Tennant, Nilfisk, Kärcher Pro; mostly mechanical but increasingly firmware-controlled. Same record-breadth purpose.

These are examples, not a menu of separate asks. Each contributes to the record showing the same TPM-gating-repair pattern across the full surface of commercial-premises equipment.

Critical exclusions
  • Life-safety systems — fire alarm, smoke control, intrusion detection, elevator interlock, sprinkler controls. Carved out entirely.
  • Any modification that changes grid-interconnect behavior, safety setpoints, or certified parameters (UL 1741, IEEE 1547).
  • Modifications that affect HVAC's regulated efficiency or emissions parameters (EPA, DOE).

Note for the opposition: the 2017 §1201 Study explicitly directs safety arguments away from copyright: "to the extent that commenters oppose an exemption for repair out of non‐copyright related concerns, such as public safety, the Office believes these matters are better addressed through laws or regulations outside of the Copyright Act" (PDF p. 94). Our carve-outs above implement that direction; safety regulation belongs to UL, IEEE, EPA, DOE, and AHJ inspection — not §1201.

Why now
Bricking incidents and service-monopoly lockouts have crossed from anecdote to documented pattern. SunPower bankruptcy left tens of thousands of homeowners with locked monitoring; EV-charger network failures (ChargePoint, EVgo, Blink) leave owners and operators unable to restore service; commercial HVAC service-exclusivity is documented across BOMA-member buildings; commercial-laundry vendor lockouts are documented in trade publications and across the operator-association record. The 2017 §1201 Study itself characterized the rulemaking as a "somewhat ill-fitting regulatory approach" for cross-cutting repair issues (PDF p. 92, quoting former Register Peters) — exactly the issue this broad class is designed to address.
Unified user community
Operators of commercial-premises equipment: commercial-property owners, BOMA / IFMA members, hospital and university facilities staff, hotel and hospitality operators, laundromat and small-commercial operators, gym and fitness-center owners, energy operators, EV-charging-network operators, and the independent service organizations (ISOs) that serve all of them. The user is defined by what they own and operate, not by what specific equipment category we pick out. The Office's preferred "cohesive user community" test is satisfied.
Precedent
2024 §201.40(b)(13) vehicle/vessel (vehicles have safety-critical components; the Office accepted "restore original specifications" repair); (b)(16) food-prep (commercial-operator user community); (b)(17) medical devices (related-data-files pattern); (b)(15)'s home-systems leg (analog for the residential side of the same equipment categories). All four 2024 grants support the framing.
Coalition lead
BOMA (Building Owners and Managers Association) + SEIA (Solar Energy Industries Association).
Co-leads
IFMA (International Facility Management Association); ChargePoint and fleet-operator coalitions; local-solar advocates (Solar United Neighbors); IREC (Interstate Renewable Energy Council); NFIB on the small-commercial-operator side; independent gym, laundromat, and hospitality operator associations; commercial-laundry trade groups.
Expected opposition
Tesla (EV chargers + solar + batteries combined), Enphase, SolarEdge; Carrier, Trane, Daikin, Honeywell, Johnson Controls (commercial HVAC); Hobart (dishwashers); Alliance/Speed Queen on commercial laundry. Many; opposition surface is broader than narrower-class options would produce. The strategic principle: opposition breadth is not the problem — record depth is the answer.
Record-building plan
Two-witness-per-category target: 2+ commercial HVAC operators with documented lockouts; 2+ BMS service technicians at non-OEM service companies; 2+ solar installers (SunPower-aftermath, abandoned-vendor); 2+ EV-charger operators (including fleet); 2+ commercial-laundry operators; 1+ each from commercial-dishwasher / fitness / cleaning / hospitality categories. Plus: 1+ grid-safety engineer to validate the safety carve-outs; 1+ ISO industry representative covering the cross-category service-monopoly pattern. The record-depth across categories is what wins this filing.
Risk
Medium-high. The variable here is record depth, not class breadth. The 2017 Study's "ill-fitting rulemaking" framing and the 2017 recommendation against device-specific limitations (PDF p. 94) both support a broad class. A thin record at Round 1 — not the breadth itself — is what would lose this. Plan the next 8 months around evidence development.
Expected outcome
Single new (b)(N) for software-enabled commercial-premises equipment, with explicit life-safety and grid-interconnect carve-outs. Office may codify in two paragraphs (e.g., one for HVAC/BMS, one for energy/EV) — pre-position with that fallback. EV charging and commercial HVAC are the highest-confidence sub-categories; solar/battery face heaviest opposition; all are supported under the unified frame.

Parking lot — smaller / not-yet-formalized candidates

These came up in scoping but are not in the 5-filing list either because (a) they are plausibly already covered by an existing class and only need testing, (b) they lack a coalition lead, or (c) they would dilute a more-focused filing. Worth a re-look before petitions are frozen.

Hearing aids — post-2022 OTC

The FDA's August 2022 OTC rule created a consumer market that didn't exist in any prior §1201 cycle. Fitting and pairing locked behind manufacturer software (Phonak Target, Oticon Genie); battery and shell repair locked. Plausibly already reached by (b)(17) medical-device (prescription side) or (b)(15) consumer-device (OTC side) — but never specifically tested on the record. A short clarifying filing could resolve the ambiguity.

Drones — particularly commercial / professional

DJI-dominated market with parts authentication, battery-pack pairing, motor/ESC pairing. Consumer drones plausibly reach (b)(15); commercial drones (ag mapping, surveying, professional cinematography) fall into the same commercial/industrial gap that swallowed the 2024 Class 5 broad ask. Could be folded into Filing 3 if a coalition lead emerges.

Smart power tools & yard equipment with paired batteries

Milwaukee, DeWalt, EGO, etc. Battery authentication + tool-pairing chips. Consumer-grade plausibly covered by (b)(15); the contractor / job-site tier, which dominates the market, sits in the commercial/industrial gap. Candidate to fold into Filing 1 (construction) if scope permits.

E-readers — repair (not jailbreak)

Kindle jailbreaking was denied 2015 for interoperability purposes. Repair (battery replacement de-authentication, paired screens) is a separate question that has never been on the §1201 record. Probably folded into (b)(15) consumer-devices on its face, but untested.

Personal computers / laptops — now absorbed into Filing 4

Originally a parking-lot item refreshing the 2012 SFLC PC-repair denial. Now part of Filing 4 (General-Purpose Computing & Networking), which targets the gap that (b)(15)'s "primarily designed for consumers" gate leaves for PCs / Macs / workstations in commercial use.

Strategic notes from the record

The "broad class" objection is pretext for "thin record." The Office uses non-cohesive-class language as cover when it wants to deny but doesn't want to commit to a substantive holding. The actual decision variable is record depth: classes that lost the broad-class objection (2021 Class 12, 2024 Class 5 broader scope) also had thin records; classes that won despite breadth (vehicles 2015, consumer devices 2018, medical devices 2021) had concrete, named-witness, named-incident records. Counter the broad-class objection by building the record, not by shrinking scope. And cite the 2017 §1201 Study at PDF p. 94: the Office "recommends against limiting an exemption to specific technologies or devices" — that's the Office's own view that breadth is desirable.

Specific examples build the record; they don't argue for separate filings. When a filing names twelve equipment categories under one class, each category is a record-depth contribution. The diversity of examples is the argument that the class needs to be broad. Splitting into twelve filings would mis-frame what is a single systemic problem.

Round 1 comments (~Dec 2026) is the deadline that matters, not the petition itself. The petition is short. Plan the next 6 months around Round 1 — that's where the record is built.

Treat denials as 2030 raw material. Every "insufficient record" denial tells you exactly what evidence to develop for the next cycle. The 2024 food-prep grant was built on the 2021 commercial/industrial denial — same play, run again at scale here.

The Register's permanent-exemption recommendation is the political backdrop. The 2017 §1201 Study recommended Congress adopt a permanent statutory repair exemption (PDF pp. 88–96). Congress has not acted. That recommendation is still sitting there — every triennial repair win builds the case that the rulemaking is doing work Congress should have made permanent (and which the Freedom To Fix Act would do directly). Cite the Register's own recommendation whenever opposition argues a class is "too broad" or "should wait for legislation": the Office's own view is that the rulemaking is "ill-fitting" for these issues and that broader, device-agnostic protection is what the statute eventually needs. Triennial wins are the bridge.

Deferred to 2030

Professional A/V / broadcast / cinema equipment. Distinct copyright concerns (these devices process audiovisual works); needs careful framing around device-control firmware that does NOT touch access-to-works. Should be its own filing with its own coalition (Society of Broadcast Engineers, post-production trade groups). Not included now to avoid diluting the five focused 2027 filings.
